Talks · 14 August 2026 · FuturAIma Summer School, Neuchâtel

You are already living in an agentic world

You are already living in an agentic world, you just do not know it yet. That was the title of the last talk of the summer school, and this is that talk written out.

I had three goals for the hour. First, to put a name on systems you already know and have already used, and to explain briefly how they work. Second, to describe how those systems have changed the daily professional lives of computational scientists, and how they are likely to change other scientists’ lives too. Third, to give you a concrete mindset change, some pieces of advice, and enough understanding of the tooling to improve your own work with these systems, starting today.

A language model on its own: one prompt, one answer

Before talking about agentic systems, I want to say that these systems are built on large language models. I think everyone knows by now what a large language model is, but as a quick reminder: it is a model you give a prompt and a context, so in the end some text, and it gives you back an answer, also as text.

To show that, and to explain what an agentic system is later, I am going to draw the language model as a robot. His name is Clank, after my favourite childhood robot.

You can imagine that you, the human, go to him with some context, which is the block of papers here, you hand it over with a prompt, which is the speech bubble, and he gives you back an answer that you can keep. Once he has done that, he stops, and nothing happens. That is really what an interaction with a large language model is like.

You can repeat it, once, twice, and it is the same thing every time. You give a prompt with context, you get an answer. Nothing is carried over from the time before.

One word: autonomy

That brings us to agentic systems. To show what an agent is, take the example of watering plants.

Take our language model, Clank. He is standing in the room, not doing anything. The human comes in and asks him to water the plants. Clank works out what the request means, then goes to each plant and waters it. When he is finished, he goes back to the human, says it is done, and the human leaves, because the task is over.

That is what agentic systems are. They are systems built on a large language model that receive a task, decide on their own workflow and how they are going to get the task done, interact with their environment, and come back to you when they are finished. The word I want you to remember is autonomy. They are able to decide by themselves how they are going to finish the task, and how they know the task is done.

They follow a loop, which we call the agentic loop:

  • First they get the task from you, understand it, and then observe their environment.
  • They think about what to do.
  • They use their tools to carry the action out.
  • They observe what happened when they used the tools.
  • They check whether the job is done. If it is, they leave the loop. If it is not, they go back to the thinking part and round again.

You can read Clank the same way:

  • He got the task from me, understood it, and then observed his environment to find the plants.
  • He thought about what to do to water them.
  • He walked to the plants and used the tool to water them.
  • He observed that all the plants had been watered, decided that was a good enough job, reported back to me, and went on standby.

That is the basis of how agentic systems work.

And here is what you may not have realised: systems like ChatGPT and Claude are agentic systems now, not simple language models. When you give them a task, they understand it, they look at the set of tools they have, and they decide which of those tools would be best for the job.

Say the task is to pull some data off the internet and show it in a figure. The tools would be web search and coding. ChatGPT looks the data up online, gets the result of its search, looks at it, and decides whether it did a good job. Maybe it did not search properly, so it tries other keywords. Then it gets the data back, looks at it, decides it is good, and checks its plan. There is still the figure to make, so it uses the programming tool to generate the plot, looks at the result and thinks about it, notices that it did not use the right colours or the right bin sizes, updates the code, generates the plot again, looks at it, decides it has now done a good enough job, leaves the loop and shows you the output.

That is conceptually how ChatGPT works, and that is what agentic systems are.

It already happened, in computational science

Even though it may not look like it to some people, agentic systems are getting bigger by the day, they are being put to work in more and more fields, and they are set to change the way we work fundamentally.

That change has already happened in software development and in computational science. We used to write all the code ourselves, including looking up how to use a particular library. We did all the debugging when the code did not work, and on a big codebase that could take days. All of that is gone now. AI agents write the code, write tests for it to make sure it works properly, and debug it when it does not. It generally goes much faster than a human, because the agent has constant knowledge of the whole codebase.

Because all of that is now done by the agents, our role converted from doing those things to focusing on research, decision making and validation. It also means that most of the time we can work on several things at once: take a decision, let the agent work on it, make sure the code and the results are right, and meanwhile move to another project, going back and forth between two or more.

And you run several of these at once.

So the role of the computational scientist changed quite drastically, and it happened quite quickly. In 2021 the first AI pair programmer shipped as a preview. It was buggy, it made mistakes, and it still helped a little. Now, five years later, we have agentic systems that are technically capable of doing complete projects and complete sets of experiments on their own, from start to finish, testing everything and logging their work properly. That is true across many fields of computational science, not only ours.

Self-driving labs, already being built

I do not know how much you have heard about self-driving labs. These are labs where everything is done by robots, with only minimal human intervention. There have been efforts to build them for quite some time, mostly driven by academic labs, but we are seeing more and more examples from academic labs and from industry.

  • MicroCycleNovartis, Basel

    Plate-based micro-scale chemistry of compounds chosen with active learning, automated purification and in-situ quantification.

    J. Med. Chem. · 2024 · 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.3c02029

  • MinervaRoche Basel and EPFL

    A machine learning framework that designs highly parallel 96-well experiments against several objectives at once, validated and then deployed in real process development.

    Nature Communications · 2025 · 10.1038/s41467-025-61803-0

  • Two mobile robotsCooper Group, University of Liverpool

    An automated synthesis platform, a liquid-chromatography mass spectrometer and a benchtop NMR, with the robots deciding what to synthesise next from the measurements.

    Nature · 2024 · 10.1038/s41586-024-08173-7

  • One planner, five labsAcceleration Consortium, Toronto

    A cloud-based experiment planner drove five geographically separate robotic platforms as independent asynchronous workers across the world, and produced 21 new best-in-class gain materials for organic solid-state lasers.

    Science · 2024 · 10.1126/science.adk9227

  • SAMPLEUniversity of Wisconsin-Madison

    Software agents each learned their own sequence-to-function model and each designed enzyme variants, and a robotic system built, expressed and assayed them.

    Nature Chemical Engineering · 2024 · 10.1038/s44286-023-00002-4

  • Tableting data factoryCMAC Strathclyde, AstraZeneca and UCB

    A predictive model picks the formulation, then a robotic line doses, blends with in-line monitoring, compacts, and tests every single tablet for weight, thickness, diameter and breaking force.

    Nature Communications · 2026 · 10.1038/s41467-026-71204-6

Several of these are teams describing their own systems, which is worth saying out loud rather than leaving out.

And while this sounds futuristic, it is to be taken with a grain of salt, because when you read the publications they all say these are proofs of concept at best, and they each have their own caveats. Roche wrote, about their own high-throughput chemistry, that “in productive use, humans continue to design the overwhelming majority of HTE plates”.

So the reason I am showing them is not to say that we have reached a time where self-driving labs exist. Not at all. What I want to show is the direction the field is moving in, because that direction is becoming more and more concrete, and I think you should be aware of it.

Self-driving labs are not here. Autonomous ones are close.

The section above was honest: self-driving labs are proofs of concept, and the papers say so themselves. But that is a claim about labs that decide what to run next. It is not a claim about labs that run things without hands, and those are a different thing, and those are already here.

So, plainly: self-driving labs are not here yet. Autonomous labs are. One is a research question. The other is a product you could buy time on this afternoon.

Now look at what is actually in there. Fixed, purpose-built machines doing one job each, in a cage, on a floor plan somebody designed around them. They cannot adapt. If a tube is in the wrong place, or a cap is stiff, or something spills, the whole thing stops and waits for a person. That is a fair criticism and I would rather make it myself, because it is what a room of bench scientists is already thinking.

But it is an argument about today’s hardware, not about the direction. The robotics field is moving quickly too, and the hands are the part moving fastest. So the honest position is this: the software layer is here, the coordination layer is here, and the part that still cannot cope with a surprise is the part with the most improvement left in it.

The hands are getting good, fast, and cheap

When it comes to the software, we are already very close. People often think this transition will never reach the physical world, because the hardware is not ready. That is true, but there is significant progress in robotics that should not be ignored. The thing I find quite cool is the ORCA hand project from ETH Zürich.

Robots are not only improving dramatically in capability and speed, they are also getting cheaper and more accessible to labs, which I believe is a key indicator of the direction the world is heading in.

What is left, and what stays ours

This is the good part, so let me frame it as the opportunity it is. The dull, repetitive “just implementing” moments go to the machine, and your time goes to the parts you came into research for: the ideas, the judgement, the calls. The work changes shape and concentrates into judgement.

Read it top to bottom. The three on the run are not three separate jobs, they are the three places a person touches one piece of work: before it, on the way back, and at the end. The band between them is the machine’s part, and it is deliberately the quietest thing on the picture, because the doing is no longer the interesting part.

Then the one below the rule, which is the one to slow down on. The first three are the ones everybody expects. The fourth is the one that is actually new: it is not that you work harder at staying sharp, it is that a different job appears, and it is a job worth having. The people who understand how these systems actually work will be the ones coordinating them. Not only brainstorming and validating, but fixing what breaks, with a technician beside them, and buying in the capability the building has not got by connecting to somewhere that has it. Nobody is hiring for that job by that name yet, which is exactly why starting now is worth something.

Then the turn, and I want to be careful and honest here. I will not tell you that understanding is the one thing a machine never does. Nobody can show you a “never”, and an AI has already caught another AI’s mistakes. Here is the better version: however advanced it gets, an AI is still a tool like any other, and a tool never carries responsibility. Responsibility always returns to the person using it.

A tool never carries responsibility. You do.

What to point it at, whether to trust what it hands back, and who answers when it ships. The last one is yours to keep, not because a better model cannot reach it, but because being answerable is a status a person carries, not a skill a model gets better at.

Your field already runs on this. The Qualified Person certifies each batch for release. And since 2 August 2026, human oversight of high-risk AI is a live legal duty in the EU, under Article 14 of the AI Act.

I cannot teach you the tools. They change too fast.

So the honest question is what to do about all this on Monday.

The reason first: because of the direction all of this is going, you need to know how to use these tools. Not eventually. Now, while the gap between the people who do and the people who do not is still small.

Then let me be honest about what one hour can be. I cannot possibly teach you everything in one session, and anyone who tells you they can is selling something. What I can do is three things, and they are worth very different amounts.

All three start today, and the only question is how far each of them gets.

What is possible today runs out first, and the reason is plain: it is out of date before you have finished learning it. The dashed line is that room, a year from now, at the next summer school. That track has already ended by then.

A few habits run well past it, because a habit does not belong to the tool it was learned on.

A way of thinking and working has no end in sight, which is why that track does not stop, it just fades out. There is no point on the picture where it ends, because there is no point where it ends. That third track is the reason I was up there. If you forget the rest, that is fine.

Treat it like someone you have just hired

This is the first of two mindset changes, and it is the part I actually came to say. Everything else was tools and habits, and tools and habits go out of date. These two do not.

Think of the agent you are working with as an employee. Somebody you have just hired, who is fast and well read and knows nothing about how your lab works.

Now the part that matters. If they are not doing a good job, you do not just accept it. Nobody would accept it from a person. You would work out why, and you would fix it. Either they are missing something they need, so you give them the tools, or they do not know how you want it done, so you teach them.

If it is doing a bad job, that is a problem to fix. Never a verdict to accept.

So never, ever accept that it is failing. The commonest thing I see is somebody trying an agent once, getting something mediocre, deciding these things are overrated, and going back to doing it by hand. That is the equivalent of hiring somebody, watching them get one thing wrong on their first morning, and never speaking to them again.

It is a bad tool right up until you make it a good one, and making it a good one is your job, not the vendor’s.

When you hit a wall, ask what could be done about it

This is the second mindset change, and it is the one I would keep if I could only keep one.

Develop this reflex: whenever you feel a barrier in your work, ask an agentic model what could be done about it. Not later, not when you have time. At the moment you feel the friction.

The reason is that you are almost certainly wrong about what is possible. All of us are. We each decided once what our tools could and could not do, and then we stopped checking, and meanwhile the answer changed. So do not stay inside your own estimate of what is achievable, because there is far more available than any of us assumes.

Be honest about what happens when you do this, because it goes one of two ways and both are wins. Either you are surprised, and there is a workflow that solves the thing outright. Or there is not, and you come away understanding your own problem better and knowing how to make it a bit less bad. There is no third outcome where you wasted your time.

Now the part people get wrong. This is not about finding the perfect workflow that dissolves your problem. That is rare. It is about improving one tiny part of how you work, every day. One tiny part a day compounds into something you would not recognise in a year, and waiting for the big solution gets you nothing at all.

Not to solve everything. To make one small part of next week better than this one.

So never accept a situation you find suboptimal. Everything can always get better, and the only question is whether you asked.

Start with how you ask

The rest of the talk was five things to try when an agent is not doing a good enough job, cheapest first. You only climb when the one below did not work.

The band in colour is where we start, and it is the cheapest of the lot, because it needs nothing plugged in and nothing installed. It is just how you ask. Both of the first two work this afternoon, on whatever you already have open.

One: give it a finish line it can check

Say how you will know the job is done, and give it a way to test its own work against that line.

This is why the loop comes back. Look at the disc marked one, on the check step: that box is the only reason the loop ever stops. With no finish line it exits on its own judgement of good enough, and its judgement of good enough is not yours. Give it the line and it keeps going round until the line is met.

Your field already runs on exactly this. A docked pose is trusted once it passes a physical-validity check. A chromatography result is trusted once the instrument passes system suitability. A synthetic route is trusted once every building block is purchasable.

And the honest other half: knowing where no such line exists is the judgement that stays with you.

Two: brief it like a colleague

Unlearn Google. Asking an agent is not a keyword search, and that reflex is exactly why people get poor results out of it. Full sentences, the context it cannot see, an example of what good looks like. On the diagram that is the disc marked two, which is the task going in, so briefing well is a change to what enters the loop.

If you would not send that message to a new colleague and expect a useful answer back, do not send it here either.

The objection to this is always the same: that is a lot of typing. It is not, and in the room I demonstrated it rather than argued about it. I had been dictating the whole time I was explaining these two habits, using a tool called Whisper Flow, and I pasted the transcript onto the wall. I did not write a word of it. I briefed it the way I would brief a person, and it took me no longer than saying it out loud, because saying it out loud is all I did.

Wanting to type less is not a reason to ask badly.

Then reach for the right tool

There are two ways up here, and they are different axes. Either somebody has already built a tool around the job, so you go and use that one instead of a general chat. Or the tool you have is right and it simply cannot reach something, so you plug that in.

Three: use the tool that already has the capability

You do not plug anything in. Somebody has already wired an agent to one job and given it the right hands for that job. This is the easy route, and most people will use it before they ever touch a connector.

Everything you are about to see comes from something I actually made. Nothing is a mock-up.

The example is Claude Design. It is not a chat window and it is not an image generator. It is a design tool with a model inside it, and what comes out is an editable design rather than a picture of one. It does slides, documents, posters, diagrams, wireframes and a CV. You describe what you want, you pick a design system, you choose the model, and it starts.

What I asked it for was a flier for an industry day at a pharma company down the road. Here is the whole thing as it came out. It set the type, the grid and the colour, kept both institutions’ marks and placed them properly, sized and cropped the photographs into the layout, and put the details where they belong. Nothing on it was drawn by hand.

This next part is the beat that separates it from asking a chatbot for a picture. I selected one block, the top of the flier, and left a comment on it, exactly the way you would comment on a colleague’s draft. The comment is in plain language and it is not even spelled correctly:

Can you please lower the position of the spots vailable box here so that it fills the space better? Can you also please align “30” and “spots available” in a better way?

It did that, and only that. I checked it from the pixels rather than by eye: the only thing that differs anywhere on the page is a box 281 by 101 pixels, which is the badge. Everything else, down to the photographs, is identical.

Read that against the loop: this is the agent given one instruction about one part. I was in that loop for about ten seconds and I changed the outcome. I did not start over, and I did not have to describe the whole flier again. That is what working with one of these actually feels like on a good day. You point at the one thing that is wrong.

The other thing to believe is that this is not an image. A word in the body copy, the words inside the badge, and the date at the foot of the flier typed into a different format, each with an ordinary formatting toolbar open above it.

The text is live text. So are the colours, the type and the spacing. Nothing is baked into a picture.

That matters for a reason you will feel immediately: a poster you cannot edit is a poster you have to regenerate, and regenerating means everything else moves too. A date changes. One always does.

And it remembers your other projects, which is how a group ends up with a house style it actually keeps to, without anyone policing it.

You spend the effort once, on the design, and after that a new poster is “make this one, in the design of that one”.

The honest caveat: it is very good at layout and typography, and it is not a designer. You are still the one who decides it is finished. And the general lesson under all of this is the third piece of advice: before you try to make a general chat do a job, look for the tool that was built for it.

It plugs into other systems, and there are hundreds

That is the second way, and it is the one that matters most for your actual work. There will never be a purpose-built tool for your particular corner of pharmacy. So the other route is to take the ordinary chat you already use, and plug into it exactly the capabilities your work needs.

The name matters, because the name is what you have to search for. In ChatGPT these are called apps. In Claude they are called connectors. Underneath both is one open standard, MCP, now under the Linux Foundation, with OpenAI and Microsoft supporting it too. Write the connector once and every agent can use it, which is why there are already hundreds.

The left and right of this picture is the whole point. Everything on the left only ever informs it: after the query, the world is exactly as it was. Everything on the right lets it act: after one of those, something out there is different. That is not a category, it is an observable fact about what got left behind.

Everything on the left makes it better informed. Everything on the right makes it accountable, to you.

One thing to say out loud: the chemistry connectors are community-built wrappers around the public APIs, not official products of the databases. They work, and you check what you plug in, the same as any tool. Business connectors exist too, and they are the same eight shapes pointed at different systems rather than new kinds.

Four: before you struggle, check whether a connector already exists

The mistake is not that people fail to use connectors. It is that they struggle through the job by hand first, pasting tables in, copying numbers out, describing a paper they could have handed over, and only afterwards find out that the thing they needed already existed and took two minutes to switch on.

So make it a reflex, before the struggle rather than after it. Three places to look:

  • In the app. Both ChatGPT and Claude have a directory you can browse.
  • In a registry. There is a public list of MCP servers, and most vendors keep their own.
  • Ask it. It knows its own tool list, so this answer is one you can trust in a way that asking it for facts is not.

Then the honest limit: what you plug in is yours to check, the same as any other tool you would put in a method.

Today a connector is a website. It does not have to be.

Today a connector is a website. A database, a search engine, a notebook, a repository. There is nothing in the idea that says it has to be.

Picture your own building. One connector per instrument. One per assay. One per part of the lab. The plate reader. The purification station. The synthesiser. The freezer inventory. The agent stops being a thing you chat to and becomes the one place that coordinates the whole of it.

Then the line that leaves the building, which is the genuinely new part. What your building has not got, you connect to a building that has. You do not own the instrument. You reach it, and you pay by the run.

This is not a thought experiment. The same Ginkgo Bioworks whose lab is further up this page opened exactly this to outside groups in March 2026: over seventy instruments reachable from a browser, where you submit a protocol in plain language and get back a price for running it. Emerald Cloud Lab has worked this way for years. There are people building the business model right now.

And one more line for the design tool: you can plug those same connectors into it, so a poster or a figure is built from your real numbers rather than from numbers you typed in again.

None of that needs a new idea. It needs the connectors to be written.

The same loop, around a whole project

This is the one to slow down on. Everything so far has been pieces: how to ask, which tool, what to plug in. This is what a whole project looks like when all of it is connected, and it is the same loop you saw at the very beginning, drawn around a project instead of around a single task.

This is the finished version. Not a demo, not next year’s roadmap. The way this works end to end.

  • What is known. Not what you remember: every run you have ever done, including the ones that failed, which are the ones nobody ever goes back and reads. Plus the literature, searched properly, as a corpus.
  • The protocol to try. This is the teal box, and it is the important one. It does not hand you one protocol. It looks at your own past trials and at the literature and gives you options, each with the case for it and the case against it. And you pick the one to run.
  • Make and measure. Automated synthesis, purification, characterisation. In this picture that is a connector like any other: the platform does it and hands the numbers back.
  • The result, read and analysed, and written into your electronic notebook as it happens rather than three weeks later from memory.

Then the fork, which is the point of the whole picture. Is it good enough? You choose. Either you stop, or you go round again, back to what is known, now with one more run in it.

So there are exactly two places on that diagram where a person acts, and they are the two teal marks: which option to run, and when to stop. Everything else the system does.

And now the aside. Look at that loop again and count what you cannot do this afternoon. One box. Make and measure. Everything else on that picture works today with the things I have shown you. You just walk to the bench yourself in the middle of it.

So start now, and here is the honest reason why. Not because it saves you a morning, though it will. Because the day that box goes too, and you have seen how close it is, the jump will be nothing to you. You will already know how to brief one of these, how to give it a finish line, what to plug into it, and how to tell when it is wrong. The people who start when the platform arrives will be learning both at once. You get to learn the hard half now, on the part that already works.

Five: and if none of that fixes it, teach it

Up the ladder from the bottom: give it a finish line, brief it properly, use the tool that already has the capability, plug in what it needs to reach. Then the new one, at the top. If none of that fixes it, teach it.

The easy version, and everybody can do this today. Whenever you notice it getting the same thing wrong, do not correct it again in the next message, because that correction dies with the conversation. Write the rule into its instructions, once, and it stops. Mine says things like which spelling to use, and never to invent a citation.

The bigger version, for something that is a procedure rather than a preference: write it a skill. A skill is a page of instructions it opens only when the job needs it, so you can teach it something long and detailed without that sitting in every conversation you ever have. If your group has a way of doing a particular analysis, that is a skill, and once it is written everybody in the group has it.

None of the five is technical. They are all about being clear with it, and about not accepting a bad job.

The two that do not go out of date

Everything between the mindset changes and here was the ladder: how you ask, reaching for the right tool, teaching it. All five of those only matter if you refuse to accept a bad job in the first place. It is a bad tool right up until you make it a good one, and making it a good one is your job.

And the one I would keep if I could only keep one. Everything in that hour goes out of date. This does not. Whenever you feel a barrier, ask an agentic model what could be done about it, at the moment you feel it. Either you are surprised, or you understand your own problem better. There is no third outcome.

Never accept a situation you find suboptimal. Everything can always get better, and the only question is whether you asked.

Come and talk to me about it

These questions are a daily part of my life. I think about them, I argue about them, and I am genuinely happy to talk about them with anybody who wants to.

So come and find me, in person or by mail, whenever you like. If you want to work out what this would mean for your particular project, that conversation is far more useful than anything I could have put on a slide, because it can be about your actual work. And the specific offer, because a general invitation gets taken up by nobody and a specific one gets taken up: if you want to sit down and set one of these up properly for your own work, ask me. That is an hour, and it is an hour that changes your next year.

Now you do

You are already living in an agentic world. That was the title, and the title said you just do not know it yet. Now you do. That is the whole talk.

The tools will keep changing and the mindset will not, so start now, on something small, this week.
