You are already living in an agentic world, you just do not know it yet. That was the title of the
last talk of the summer school, and this is that talk written out.

I had three goals for the hour. First, to put a name on systems you already know and have already
used, and to explain briefly how they work. Second, to describe how those systems have changed the
daily professional lives of computational scientists, and how they are likely to change other
scientists' lives too. Third, to give you a concrete mindset change, some pieces of advice, and
enough understanding of the tooling to improve your own work with these systems, starting today.

## A language model on its own: one prompt, one answer

Before talking about agentic systems, I want to say that these systems are built on **large language
models**. I think everyone knows by now what a large language model is, but as a quick reminder: it
is a model you give a **prompt** and a **context**, so in the end some text, and it gives you back
an answer, also as text.

To show that, and to explain what an agentic system is later, I am going to draw the language model
as a robot. His name is Clank, after my favourite childhood robot.

You can imagine that you, the human, go to him with some context, which is the block of papers here,
you hand it over with a prompt, which is the speech bubble, and he gives you back an answer that you
can keep. Once he has done that, **he stops, and nothing happens.** That is really what an
interaction with a large language model is like.

You can repeat it, once, twice, and it is the same thing every time. You give a prompt with context,
you get an answer. Nothing is carried over from the time before.

## One word: autonomy

That brings us to **agentic systems**. To show what an agent is, take the example of watering
plants.

Take our language model, Clank. He is standing in the room, not doing anything. The human comes in
and asks him to water the plants. Clank works out what the request means, then goes to each plant
and waters it. When he is finished, he goes back to the human, says it is done, and the human
leaves, because the task is over.

That is what agentic systems are. They are systems built on a large language model that **receive a
task**, **decide on their own workflow** and how they are going to get the task done, **interact
with their environment**, and **come back to you** when they are finished. The word I want you to
remember is **autonomy**. They are able to decide by themselves *how* they are going to finish the
task, and *how they know* the task is done.

They follow a loop, which we call **the agentic loop**:

- First they **get the task** from you, understand it, and then **observe their environment**.
- They **think** about what to do.
- They **use their tools** to carry the action out.
- They **observe** what happened when they used the tools.
- They **check whether the job is done**. If it is, they leave the loop. If it is not, they go back
  to the thinking part and round again.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/agentic-loop.svg"}
  alt="The agentic loop, drawn as four boxes going clockwise: a task arrives at Think, what to do next; Think leads to Act, use a tool; Act leads to Observe, what happened; Observe leads to Done, check against the goal, which either returns to Think or hands the result back to you."
  plate
/>

You can read Clank the same way:

- He got the task from me, understood it, and then observed his environment to find the plants.
- He thought about what to do to water them.
- He walked to the plants and used the tool to water them.
- He observed that all the plants had been watered, decided that was a good enough job, reported
  back to me, and went on standby.

That is the basis of how agentic systems work.

And here is what you may not have realised: systems like **ChatGPT and Claude are agentic systems
now, not simple language models.** When you give them a task, they understand it, they look at the
set of tools they have, and they decide which of those tools would be best for the job.

Say the task is to pull some data off the internet and show it in a figure. The tools would be **web
search** and **coding**. ChatGPT looks the data up online, gets the result of its search, looks at
it, and decides whether it did a good job. Maybe it did not search properly, so it tries other
keywords. Then it gets the data back, looks at it, decides it is good, and checks its plan. There is
still the figure to make, so it uses the programming tool to generate the plot, looks at the result
and thinks about it, notices that it did not use the right colours or the right bin sizes, updates
the code, generates the plot again, looks at it, decides it has now done a good enough job, leaves
the loop and shows you the output.

That is conceptually how ChatGPT works, and that is what agentic systems are.

## It already happened, in computational science

Even though it may not look like it to some people, agentic systems are getting bigger by the day,
they are being put to work in more and more fields, and they are set to change the way we work
fundamentally.

That change has already happened in software development and in computational science. We used to
write all the code ourselves, including looking up how to use a particular library. We did all the
**debugging** when the code did not work, and on a big codebase that could take days. All of that is
gone now. AI agents write the code, write tests for it to make sure it works properly, and debug it
when it does not. It generally goes much faster than a human, because the agent has constant
knowledge of the whole codebase.

Because all of that is now done by the agents, **our role converted** from doing those things to
focusing on research, decision making and validation. It also means that most of the time we can
work on several things at once: take a decision, let the agent work on it, make sure the code and
the results are right, and meanwhile move to another project, going back and forth between two or
more.

<Figure

In 2026 the middle three, write, test and debug, are one block belonging to the agent, and research, decide and validate are still the person's."

> And you run **several of these at once.**

So the role of the computational scientist changed quite drastically, and it happened quite quickly.
In **2021** the first AI pair programmer shipped as a preview. It was buggy, it made mistakes, and
it still helped a little. Now, five years later, we have agentic systems that are technically
capable of doing complete projects and complete sets of experiments on their own, from start to
finish, testing everything and logging their work properly. That is true across many fields of
computational science, not only ours.

is done by robots, with only minimal human intervention. There have been efforts to build them for
quite some time, mostly driven by academic labs, but we are seeing more and more examples from
academic labs and from industry.

And while this sounds futuristic, it is to be taken with a grain of salt, because when you read the
publications they all say these are proofs of concept at best, and they each have their own caveats.
Roche wrote, about their own high-throughput chemistry, that "in productive use, humans continue to
design the overwhelming majority of HTE plates".

So the reason I am showing them is **not** to say that we have reached a time where self-driving
labs exist. Not at all. What I want to show is the **direction the field is moving in**, because
that direction is becoming more and more concrete, and I think you should be aware of it.

## Self-driving labs are not here. Autonomous ones are close.

The section above was honest: self-driving labs are proofs of concept, and the papers say so
themselves. But that is a claim about labs that **decide** what to run next. It is not a claim about
labs that **run** things without hands, and those are a different thing, and those are already here.

So, plainly: self-driving labs are not here yet. Autonomous labs are. One is a research question.
The other is a product you could buy time on this afternoon.

is the part with the most improvement left in it.

## What is left, and what stays ours

This is the good part, so let me frame it as the opportunity it is. The dull, repetitive "just
implementing" moments go to the machine, and your time goes to the parts you came into research for:
the ideas, the judgement, the calls. The work changes shape and concentrates into judgement.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/what-stays.svg"}
  alt="A run down the page: deciding what is worth doing, then a quiet band saying the machine does this part, then deciding whether to trust the result, then owning what ships. Below a rule, set apart and picked out in teal, a fourth role: coordinator, running a bench of agents and instruments."

Read it top to bottom. The three on the run are not three separate jobs, they are the three places a
person touches one piece of work: before it, on the way back, and at the end. The band between them
is the machine's part, and it is deliberately the quietest thing on the picture, because the doing
is no longer the interesting part.

Then the one below the rule, which is the one to slow down on. The first three are the ones
everybody expects. The fourth is the one that is actually new: it is not that you work harder at
staying sharp, it is that a different job appears, and it is a job worth having. The people who
understand how these systems actually work will be the ones **coordinating** them. Not only
brainstorming and validating, but fixing what breaks, with a technician beside them, and buying in
the capability the building has not got by connecting to somewhere that has it. Nobody is hiring for
that job by that name yet, which is exactly why starting now is worth something.

  src={base + "figures/hands-free.webp"}

Then the turn, and I want to be careful and honest here. I will **not** tell you that understanding
is the one thing a machine never does. Nobody can show you a "never", and an AI has already caught
another AI's mistakes. Here is the better version: however advanced it gets, an AI is still a tool
like any other, and a tool never carries responsibility. Responsibility always returns to the person
using it.

> A tool never carries responsibility. **You do.**

What to point it at, whether to trust what it hands back, and **who answers when it ships**. The
last one is yours to keep, not because a better model cannot reach it, but because being answerable
is a status a person carries, not a skill a model gets better at.

Your field already runs on this. The Qualified Person certifies each batch for release. And since 2
August 2026, human oversight of high-risk AI is a live legal duty in the EU, under Article 14 of the
AI Act.

## I cannot teach you the tools. They change too fast.

So the honest question is what to do about all this on Monday.

The reason first: because of the direction all of this is going, **you need to know how to use these
tools.** Not eventually. Now, while the gap between the people who do and the people who do not is
still small.

Then let me be honest about what one hour can be. I cannot possibly teach you everything in one
session, and anyone who tells you they can is selling something. What I can do is three things, and
they are worth very different amounts.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/what-lasts.svg"}

All three start today, and the only question is how far each of them gets.

**What is possible today** runs out first, and the reason is plain: it is out of date before you
have finished learning it. The dashed line is that room, a year from now, at the next summer school.
That track has already ended by then.

**A few habits** run well past it, because a habit does not belong to the tool it was learned on.

**A way of thinking and working** has no end in sight, which is why that track does not stop, it
just fades out. There is no point on the picture where it ends, because there is no point where it
ends. That third track is the reason I was up there. If you forget the rest, that is fine.

## Treat it like someone you have just hired

This is the first of two mindset changes, and it is the part I actually came to say. Everything else
was tools and habits, and tools and habits go out of date. These two do not.

Think of the agent you are working with as an employee. Somebody you have just hired, who is fast
and well read and knows nothing about how your lab works.

Now the part that matters. **If they are not doing a good job, you do not just accept it.** Nobody
would accept it from a person. You would work out why, and you would fix it. Either they are missing
something they need, so you give them the tools, or they do not know how you want it done, so you
teach them.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/the-new-colleague.webp"}
  alt="Two researchers at a laboratory bench in window light. The one nearer the camera, in a knitted jumper, is speaking with an open hand towards a small instrument between them; the newer colleague beside her, in a lab coat, is looking down at the instrument rather than at her."
/>

> If it is doing a bad job, that is a problem to fix. **Never a verdict to accept.**

So never, ever accept that it is failing. The commonest thing I see is somebody trying an agent
once, getting something mediocre, deciding these things are overrated, and going back to doing it by
hand. That is the equivalent of hiring somebody, watching them get one thing wrong on their first
morning, and never speaking to them again.

It is a bad tool right up until you make it a good one, and making it a good one is your job, not
the vendor's.

## When you hit a wall, ask what could be done about it

This is the second mindset change, and it is the one I would keep if I could only keep one.

Develop this reflex: whenever you feel a barrier in your work, ask an agentic model what could be
done about it. Not later, not when you have time. At the moment you feel the friction.

The reason is that you are almost certainly wrong about what is possible. All of us are. We each
decided once what our tools could and could not do, and then we stopped checking, and meanwhile the
answer changed. So do not stay inside your own estimate of what is achievable, because there is far
more available than any of us assumes.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/asking-at-the-bench.webp"}
  alt="A researcher alone at a laboratory bench, one gloved hand still holding the glassware he was working with, has raised his phone in the other and stopped mid-task to read it."
/>

Be honest about what happens when you do this, because it goes one of two ways and both are wins.
Either you are surprised, and there is a workflow that solves the thing outright. Or there is not,
and you come away understanding your own problem better and knowing how to make it a bit less bad.
There is no third outcome where you wasted your time.

Now the part people get wrong. This is **not** about finding the perfect workflow that dissolves
your problem. That is rare. It is about improving one tiny part of how you work, every day. One tiny
part a day compounds into something you would not recognise in a year, and waiting for the big
solution gets you nothing at all.

> Not to solve everything. To make **one small part** of next week better than this one.

So never accept a situation you find suboptimal. Everything can always get better, and the only
question is whether you asked.

## Start with how you ask

The rest of the talk was five things to try when an agent is not doing a good enough job, cheapest
first. You only climb when the one below did not work.

The band in colour is where we start, and it is the cheapest of the lot, because it needs nothing
plugged in and nothing installed. It is just how you ask. Both of the first two work this afternoon,
on whatever you already have open.

### One: give it a finish line it can check

Say how you will know the job is done, and give it a way to test its own work against that line.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/agentic-loop-habits.svg"}
  alt="The same agentic loop shown earlier, with a numbered disc marked one against the check step, which is filled solid to mark it as the step that decides the job is finished, and a disc marked two against the task going in."
  plate
/>

This is why the loop comes back. Look at the disc marked one, on the check step: **that box is the
only reason the loop ever stops.** With no finish line it exits on its own judgement of good enough,
and its judgement of good enough is not yours. Give it the line and it keeps going round until the
line is met.

Your field already runs on exactly this. A docked pose is trusted once it passes a physical-validity
check. A chromatography result is trusted once the instrument passes system suitability. A synthetic
route is trusted once every building block is purchasable.

And the honest other half: knowing where no such line exists is the judgement that stays with you.

### Two: brief it like a colleague

Unlearn Google. Asking an agent is not a keyword search, and that reflex is exactly why people get
poor results out of it. Full sentences, the context it cannot see, an example of what good looks
like. On the diagram that is the disc marked two, which is the task going in, so briefing well is a
change to what enters the loop.

If you would not send that message to a new colleague and expect a useful answer back, do not send
it here either.

The objection to this is always the same: that is a lot of typing. It is not, and in the room I
demonstrated it rather than argued about it. I had been dictating the whole time I was explaining
these two habits, using a tool called Whisper Flow, and I pasted the transcript onto the wall. I did
not write a word of it. I briefed it the way I would brief a person, and it took me no longer than
saying it out loud, because saying it out loud is all I did.

Wanting to type less is not a reason to ask badly.

### Three: use the tool that already has the capability

You do not plug anything in. Somebody has already wired an agent to one job and given it the right
hands for that job. This is the easy route, and most people will use it before they ever touch a

The example is Claude Design. It is not a chat window and it is not an image generator. It is a
design tool with a model inside it, and what comes out is an editable design rather than a picture
of one. It does slides, documents, posters, diagrams, wireframes and a CV. You describe what you
want, you pick a design system, you choose the model, and it starts.

## It plugs into other systems, and there are hundreds

That is the second way, and it is the one that matters most for your actual work. There will never
be a purpose-built tool for your particular corner of pharmacy. So the other route is to take the
ordinary chat you already use, and plug into it exactly the capabilities your work needs.

The name matters, because the name is what you have to search for. In ChatGPT these are called
**apps**. In Claude they are called **connectors**. Underneath both is one open standard, **MCP**,
now under the Linux Foundation, with OpenAI and Microsoft supporting it too. Write the connector
once and every agent can use it, which is why there are already hundreds.

The left and right of this picture is the whole point. Everything on the left only ever **informs**
it: after the query, the world is exactly as it was. Everything on the right lets it **act**: after
one of those, something out there is different. That is not a category, it is an observable fact
about what got left behind.

Everything on the left makes it better informed. Everything on the right makes it accountable, to
you.

One thing to say out loud: the chemistry connectors are community-built wrappers around the public
APIs, not official products of the databases. They work, and you check what you plug in, the same as
any tool. Business connectors exist too, and they are the same eight shapes pointed at different
systems rather than new kinds.

### Four: before you struggle, check whether a connector already exists

The mistake is not that people fail to use connectors. It is that they struggle through the job by
hand first, pasting tables in, copying numbers out, describing a paper they could have handed over,
and only afterwards find out that the thing they needed already existed and took two minutes to
switch on.

So make it a reflex, before the struggle rather than after it. Three places to look:

- **In the app.** Both ChatGPT and Claude have a directory you can browse.
- **In a registry.** There is a public list of MCP servers, and most vendors keep their own.
- **Ask it.** It knows its own tool list, so this answer is one you can trust in a way that asking
  it for facts is not.

Then the honest limit: what you plug in is yours to check, the same as any other tool you would put
in a method.

## Today a connector is a website. It does not have to be.

Today a connector is a website. A database, a search engine, a notebook, a repository. There is
nothing in the idea that says it has to be.

Picture your own building. One connector per instrument. One per assay. One per part of the lab. The
plate reader. The purification station. The synthesiser. The freezer inventory. The agent stops
being a thing you chat to and becomes the one place that coordinates the whole of it.

Then the line that leaves the building, which is the genuinely new part. What your building has not
got, you connect to a building that has. You do not own the instrument. You reach it, and you pay by
the run.

This is not a thought experiment. The same Ginkgo Bioworks whose lab is further up this page opened
exactly this to outside groups in March 2026: over seventy instruments reachable from a browser,
where you submit a protocol in plain language and get back a price for running it. Emerald Cloud Lab
has worked this way for years. There are people building the business model right now.

And one more line for the design tool: you can plug those same connectors into it, so a poster or a
figure is built from your real numbers rather than from numbers you typed in again.

None of that needs a new idea. It needs the connectors to be written.

## The same loop, around a whole project

This is the one to slow down on. Everything so far has been pieces: how to ask, which tool, what to
plug in. This is what a whole project looks like when all of it is connected, and it is the same
loop you saw at the very beginning, drawn around a project instead of around a single task.

This is the finished version. Not a demo, not next year's roadmap. The way this works end to end.

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/project-loop.svg"}
  alt="Four stations in a row, joined left to right, with a return running back underneath them. What is known, drawn from every run you have done including the failures and from the literature. The protocol to try, marked options you pick one, holding options drawn from both with the case for and against each. Make and measure, holding automated synthesis, purification and characterisation. And the result, read, analysed and written into your notebook. From there a fork asks whether it is good enough: you choose to stop, or to go round again with one more run in it."
  plate
/>

- **What is known.** Not what you remember: every run you have ever done, including the ones that
failed, which are the ones nobody ever goes back and reads. Plus the literature, searched properly,
as a corpus.
- **The protocol to try.** This is the teal box, and it is the important one. It does not hand you
  one protocol. It looks at your own past trials and at the literature and gives you options, each
  with the case for it and the case against it. And you pick the one to run.
- **Make and measure.** Automated synthesis, purification, characterisation. In this picture that is
  a connector like any other: the platform does it and hands the numbers back.
- **The result**, read and analysed, and written into your electronic notebook as it happens rather
than three weeks later from memory.

Then the fork, which is the point of the whole picture. Is it good enough? You choose. Either you
stop, or you go round again, back to what is known, now with one more run in it.

So there are exactly two places on that diagram where a person acts, and they are the two teal
marks: which option to run, and when to stop. Everything else the system does.

And now the aside. Look at that loop again and count what you cannot do this afternoon. **One box.**
Make and measure. Everything else on that picture works today with the things I have shown you. You
just walk to the bench yourself in the middle of it.

So start now, and here is the honest reason why. Not because it saves you a morning, though it will.
Because the day that box goes too, and you have seen how close it is, the jump will be nothing to
you. You will already know how to brief one of these, how to give it a finish line, what to plug
into it, and how to tell when it is wrong. The people who start when the platform arrives will be
learning both at once. You get to learn the hard half now, on the part that already works.

## Five: and if none of that fixes it, teach it

<Figure
  src={base + "figures/advice-outline-3.svg"}
  alt="Three stacked bands read from the bottom up, with an arrow up the side marked still not right, go up. How you ask, holding give it a finish line it can check and brief it like a colleague. Reach for the right tool, holding use the tool that already has it and plug in what it needs to reach. And at the top, picked out in teal, teach it, either with one instruction or with a written skill."
  plate
/>

Up the ladder from the bottom: give it a finish line, brief it properly, use the tool that already
has the capability, plug in what it needs to reach. Then the new one, at the top. If none of that
fixes it, teach it.

The easy version, and everybody can do this today. Whenever you notice it getting the same thing
wrong, do not correct it again in the next message, because that correction dies with the
conversation. Write the rule into its instructions, once, and it stops. Mine says things like which
spelling to use, and never to invent a citation.

The bigger version, for something that is a procedure rather than a preference: write it a skill. A
skill is a page of instructions it opens only when the job needs it, so you can teach it something
long and detailed without that sitting in every conversation you ever have. If your group has a way
of doing a particular analysis, that is a skill, and once it is written everybody in the group has
it.

None of the five is technical. They are all about being clear with it, and about not accepting a bad
job.

## The two that do not go out of date

Everything between the mindset changes and here was the ladder: how you ask, reaching for the right
tool, teaching it. All five of those only matter if you refuse to accept a bad job in the first
place. It is a bad tool right up until you make it a good one, and making it a good one is your job.

And the one I would keep if I could only keep one. Everything in that hour goes out of date. This
does not. Whenever you feel a barrier, ask an agentic model what could be done about it, at the
moment you feel it. Either you are surprised, or you understand your own problem better. There is no
third outcome.

Never accept a situation you find suboptimal. Everything can always get better, and the only
question is whether you asked.
